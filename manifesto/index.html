Manifesto

Hold the Door

Everyone has heard it, usually from somebody who loves them.

I know you love it. But it doesn’t pay, and it’s cut-throat.

They’re right. That’s the problem.

Two of us have been working musicians for thirty years. We are not describing this from the outside.

The reason isn’t talent. It’s arithmetic.

Forty tickets handed over with sell them or you’re not playing. Thirty-five dollars to enter a juried show, and thirty-five again, and thirty-five again. A painting sold in March, paid in September, and you found out from the gallery’s Instagram. Six weeks of rehearsal, unpaid, so that people could see the work. Class unpaid, rehearsal unpaid, physio out of pocket, and it’s over at thirty-one with nothing to show a landlord.

It’s never the total. It’s the gap. The money lands three months after the rent was due, every time, for twenty years. You can’t have a child on that.

Then you post about it. Smiling. Tagging the person who paid you late and paid you less, because the fee was low and you have to look delighted or you get a name for being difficult.

And you count the room. You tell yourself you won’t, and then you do. Five. One is the sound guy. One came in out of the rain. Three came on purpose, so you play it properly for the three, the thing you worked on all year, and you pack down and go home and tell nobody. Something gets smaller. You don’t notice for a year.

The damage isn’t the five. It’s the counting. Everything in this hands you a number and invites you to set it beside somebody else’s. Nobody was ever destroyed by a small room. They were destroyed by the scoreboard they carried into it.

At the end of a ballet class everybody stops and applauds. Not the dancers — the teacher, and the woman at the piano. It is part of the class. It is written into the hour, so nobody has to feel like doing it, which is exactly why it works.

Dancers have that. Painters don’t. Writers never do. Most of us make the thing alone, hand it to strangers, and let the strangers decide.

So: the first room you play is full of people who know what it took. Strangers come second. Most of you will have careers no stranger ever hears about. The people in that first room will know.

The rest of it isn’t glamorous. Nobody is making a film about someone who rings the gallery on day thirty-one, and again on day thirty-two, who reads page four of the contract, who doesn’t mind being called difficult because that is their job and not yours.

You get to be a painter, instead of a painter who is also their own publicist, street team, and the person posting at 11:40 at night because the algorithm likes 11:40 at night. You’re allowed to be only the thing you are — and the thing you are, not a flattened version of somebody more bankable. Nobody here will ask you to work in another artist’s style for a company that ends up owning it.

Artist here means everyone whose hands were on it. The engineer. The carpenter who struck the set at one in the morning. The répétiteur. The framer. Whoever fixed the hem with a hair grip and gaffer tape while the house was coming in, and told nobody.

Same membership. Same terms.

Our half

We will never take a percentage of your fee. If this needs money, it asks.

We will never hold your money. A bank holds it, in your name, apart from ours.

We will never rank you, or sell you. Your work was taken once already, to build the things that now compete with you. It will not be taken again here.

We will never put anyone in front of you in exchange for money. Not as a tier, not as a partnership, not once.

Every year we will publish what we spent, where it came from, and what we turned down and why.

If this fails, your agreements, your contacts and your record leave with you in a form anyone can read, and you work the next morning.

And the one we are least sure of: we will not grow this faster than we can keep it honest. Hold us to that one hardest.

Your half

Keep your word. Show up, deliver, pay what you agreed.

Say the name of the invisible one. Next programme, next credit, next thank-you from a stage.

Clap for whoever just went across the floor.

Take help without apologising. Give it the same way.

And when you get through, hold the door.

Somewhere tonight somebody is being told, kindly, to have a backup plan.

We are building the thing that makes that advice wrong.

Hold the door.

This site calls no one. Neither will your data.
